# Planning for Support Before Go-Live

Support is one of those things that can feel like a problem for later.

First we have to build the thing, test it, and get it deployed. Once people are actually using it, then we'll worry about what happens when something breaks.

I don't love that approach.

If people are going to depend on the technology after go-live, I want to know how they're supposed to get help before we put it in front of them.

But somebody should know what happens when the first person says, **"It's not working."**

## Give users a clear front door

The user shouldn't have to understand the org chart to report a problem.

I want a clear front door and a reasonable path behind it.

## Make the first ticket useful

There is a big difference between giving Support somewhere to escalate an issue and actually preparing them to support something.

That doesn't mean turning first-line support into engineers. It means giving them enough context that escalation adds value.

Good intake gives troubleshooting somewhere to start.

## Avoid ticket ping-pong

Support thinks it's hardware. The hardware team thinks it's software. Someone else thinks it's configuration. Eventually somebody decides it must be the vendor.

Meanwhile, the user still has the original problem.

Sometimes an issue really is difficult to diagnose and has to move between specialists. That's different from bouncing around because nobody knows who owns what.

Otherwise, the ticket lands in another queue and begins its exciting second career as somebody else's mystery ticket.

## Support tickets are implementation data

Support sees the technology under conditions the project team never completely can.

One strange ticket may just be a strange ticket. If the same error starts appearing across locations or users keep getting stuck at the same point, I want somebody to notice the pattern.

Closing the ticket solves the immediate problem.

Understanding the pattern can solve the next 20.

## Don't let Support become the workaround

If Support is manually correcting the same thing every day, we've created a recurring process and disguised it as troubleshooting.

"Support knows how to fix it" shouldn't automatically mean we're done.

## Vendors need a support path too

Vendor support shouldn't become a black hole where the internal ticket disappears until someone remembers to check on it.

The user reported the problem to us. They shouldn't have to become the project manager for their own support ticket.

## Know when stabilization is actually over

I don't think stabilization is over just because the calendar says we've reached the end of a two-week hypercare period.

I want to see that the support model is actually working.

Eventually, a new deployment should stop feeling new to the people supporting it.

Until then, I'm still paying attention to what Support is teaching us.
