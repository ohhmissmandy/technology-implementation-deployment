# Planning for Knowledge Transfer

I've spent a lot of time being the person who knew why something worked the way it did.

That's useful, but it also taught me how easily knowledge can get tied to the people who were there when decisions were made.

There's a difference between preserving information and transferring knowledge.

## Context disappears surprisingly fast

During a project, everyone close to the work carries around a lot of shared context without really noticing it.

Six months later, some of that context is already getting fuzzy.

A year later, someone may look at the exact same decision and wonder why nobody chose the obvious alternative.

Not every decision needs its entire biography preserved. But if someone is likely to revisit it later, I want to leave enough behind that they aren't starting the investigation from zero.

## "You just have to know" gets my attention

Every complicated environment develops some institutional knowledge.

I start paying attention when something important to normal operations depends on somebody saying, **"Yeah, you just have to know that."**

If people really do have to know it, how are they supposed to learn it?

## Information needs somewhere sensible to live

One giant repository containing every piece of information from an implementation sounds wonderfully organized until someone actually needs an answer.

Then they're opening folders called *Final*, *Final2*, and *Final_UseThisOne* while questioning every decision that brought them to this point.

I care less about having everything in one place than having a sensible place for the information that needs to survive.

## Some knowledge has to move between people

Not everything worth knowing fits neatly into documentation.

Sometimes it's as simple as explaining the reasoning behind a decision instead of only teaching someone which steps to follow.

Handing someone access to the files isn't the same as giving them the context they need to take ownership.

## Think about the person who isn't here yet

Eventually, people change roles. Teams reorganize. Vendors change. New employees arrive.

That person shouldn't have to perform project archaeology just to understand the environment they've inherited.

And if one person is slowly becoming the only person who understands something important, notice that before their two-week vacation becomes a business continuity event.

## The goal isn't to document everything

Trying to capture absolutely everything creates so much information that the useful parts become harder to find.

I'd rather be intentional about what needs to survive.

The people will eventually change.

The organization shouldn't have to relearn everything when they do.
