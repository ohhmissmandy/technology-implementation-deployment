# From Pilot to Deployment

I like pilots because they give you room to test assumptions, find problems early, and learn what happens when a solution leaves the planning stage and meets the real world.

A successful pilot can also give everyone a little too much confidence.

The technology worked. Users completed the process. The use case was proven. Maybe everyone walked away happy.

Great. Now do it 50 more times.

That's a different problem.

## A pilot proves that something can work

That's important, especially when you're introducing something new. A pilot can answer questions about the technology, the workflow, the user experience, and whether the original idea is worth pursuing at all.

But pilots usually happen under unusually good conditions.

Everyone is paying attention. The location may have been chosen because it was a good candidate. The project team is close by. Problems get escalated quickly. The people involved probably know they're part of something new.

None of that is bad. It's part of why pilots are useful.

It's just not what deployment at scale looks like.

## Scale removes the special treatment

The tenth location isn't going to feel like the first. By the fiftieth, it definitely shouldn't.

You can't have the same group of people personally walk every site through every step. You can't rely on someone remembering the weird thing they had to do during the pilot. And you probably don't want every small problem immediately landing with the people who built the solution.

At some point, knowledge has to become a repeatable way of working.

Sites need to know what has to be in place before rollout. Equipment needs to arrive where it belongs with the right setup. Dependencies have to be visible. Users need to know what's expected of them and Support needs to understand what they're inheriting.

At that point, we're not really asking whether the technology works anymore. We're asking whether the organization knows how to deploy it.

## Use the pilot to improve the rollout

I don't just want to know whether the pilot succeeded. I want to know what it taught us.

Maybe something took twice as long as expected. Someone had to figure out a step manually. Users kept asking the same question. A location wasn't as ready as everyone thought. Or the whole thing depended on one person knowing something nobody had bothered to write down.

Those are exactly the things I want to find before the next rollout.

If it can happen again, it probably belongs somewhere in the deployment plan. Maybe we add a readiness check or change the configuration standard. Training may need to cover something we assumed was obvious. A vendor may need to be involved earlier.

Sometimes the lesson is simply that the original approach had too many moving parts. That's useful too.

## Repeatable doesn't mean identical

Standardization matters when you're trying to scale, but I don't think a good deployment model means forcing every location or use case through the exact same path.

Real environments vary.

Security controls probably aren't optional because one site has an unusual setup. Required testing shouldn't disappear because the schedule is tight. But training may need to happen differently for one group. An installation schedule might need to change. A particular location may have a dependency that doesn't exist anywhere else.

A good rollout gives you enough structure to control what matters without pretending every implementation will be identical.

## Pay attention after go-live

The early deployments should tell you what needs to change before the next ones. If the same issue keeps appearing at different locations, that's information. The same goes for a workaround users keep inventing or something that technically works but creates far more effort than expected.

Fix the immediate problem, of course. Just don't waste the lesson.

One rough rollout can improve the next ten if somebody is paying attention.

## The goal is to need less heroics over time

Early on, the people closest to the project know everything. They understand the decisions, history, exceptions, and who to call when something goes sideways.

That makes them incredibly useful. It can also hide weaknesses in the deployment model.

If rollout only works because those people are personally holding it together, it isn't really scalable yet.

Over time, more of that knowledge should show up in clearer readiness checks, better training, useful documentation, support paths, and repeatable workflows.

A successful pilot proves the solution can work.

Scaling it means learning how to make that success much less special.
