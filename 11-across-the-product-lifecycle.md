# Across the Product Lifecycle

Working across implementation and deployment gives you an interesting view of a product over time.

You see what everyone expects before it goes live. Then you see what happens when those expectations meet actual users, locations, workflows, dependencies, and constraints.

That perspective has made me pay attention to how decisions travel.

You learn a lot by seeing what happens after a decision leaves the team that made it. Staying close to the technology over time also changes the kinds of questions you know to ask.

## Requirements look different when reality gets involved

A requirement can make perfect sense on paper and still raise new questions once someone starts figuring out how to put it into practice.

That doesn't automatically mean the requirement was wrong. It means we've learned something about what it takes to make the requirement real.

Requirements shouldn't move in one direction until they eventually become somebody else's deployment problem.

## Know which problem you're actually solving

Sometimes the product works and the rollout doesn't.

The reverse happens too.

Teams can become very good at working around something that really should change in the product.

If we don't know which problem we're solving, we can spend a lot of time fixing the wrong thing.

## Temporary decisions have surprisingly long lives

A manual step may be no big deal during a pilot.

Then the scale changes.

Now the manual configuration has to happen across hundreds of devices. The temporary spreadsheet has become critical to the workflow.

Temporary solutions have a funny way of earning permanent jobs.

The better question isn't always **"Does this work?"**

Sometimes it's **"At what point will this stop working well?"**

## What are we setting the next team up to inherit?

Every stage of the product lifecycle leaves something behind for someone else.

Physical technology makes some of this especially hard to ignore because the equipment can still be sitting somewhere years later, along with whatever setup, records, and decisions came with it.

I don't expect every decision to account for every future possibility. We'd never get anything done.

I do want to avoid solving today's problem by quietly creating a much larger one for whoever comes next.

## Replacement Has Its Own Implementation Work

Eventually, technology has to leave.

Hardware gets replaced. Systems are retired. Users move to something new. Integrations disappear and vendors change.

Replacing technology can be harder than introducing it the first time because you're changing an environment that may have spent years building workflows and dependencies around what you're removing.

People still need a plan. Existing operations have to keep working during the transition. Equipment may need to be recovered or retired. Records need to stay accurate.

You're implementing change again, this time in an environment that already has history.

I don't need to own every part of the product lifecycle to care about those connections. I've just seen enough of them to keep asking one question:

**What are we setting the next team up to inherit?**

Because eventually, there is always a next team.
