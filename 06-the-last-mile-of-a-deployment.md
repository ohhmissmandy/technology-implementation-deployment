# The Last Mile of a Deployment

There is a point in every deployment where the plan has to stop being theoretical.

The requirements are approved. Testing is complete. The equipment or software is ready. Training may be done.

Now it has to work **there**.

At that location, with those users, in whatever environment actually exists when deployment day arrives.

I've learned not to underestimate that part. A project can be completely on track and still have a rough landing because the last mile wasn't given the same attention as everything leading up to it.

## "Ready" depends on who you ask

Engineering may be ready because its work is complete. Equipment may be ready because it's configured and shipped. Operations may still be working through the workflow. Users may have completed training but don't have the access they need yet.

Every team can be telling the truth when they say they're ready while the deployment itself absolutely is not.

That's why I want readiness to mean something specific. What actually has to be true for this solution to work at this location?

## Local details matter

Standardization makes large rollouts possible, but real locations have a habit of being inconveniently real.

One site has a network limitation. Another has a physical constraint nobody knew about. A local workflow is different than expected.

You can't build a plan around every possible exception. You can build one that gives you a chance to find the important differences before they become deployment problems.

Sometimes the most useful question before go-live is simply, **"What's different here?"**

## Watch the spaces between teams

One team thinks another is handling something. A vendor finished its part but nobody is sure what happens next. The project team considers something complete while the group receiving it is still trying to understand what they're getting.

Nobody necessarily dropped the ball. Sometimes nobody was completely sure who had the ball in the first place.

I want those transitions clear before deployment.

## The date can start making decisions for you

The closer you get to deployment, the harder it can become to have an uncomfortable conversation about readiness.

Not every open issue should stop a rollout. If we waited until absolutely everything was perfect, some projects would achieve immortality before they achieved go-live.

But we should know what we're accepting.

What I don't want is for the calendar to quietly make that decision for us.

## Someone needs to own the landing

I don't like deployments that end with the equivalent of tossing the keys across the room.

The people taking ownership need to understand what they're inheriting. Known problems should actually be known. Operations needs to understand what changes for them and Support needs enough context to recognize what they're seeing.

Then I want to watch what happens for a while.

Call it stabilization, hypercare, post-go-live support, or whatever makes sense for the organization. I'm less interested in the label than I am in having a defined period where we're still paying closer attention.

I don't expect a process where nothing unexpected ever happens.

I want one that gets smarter when it does.
